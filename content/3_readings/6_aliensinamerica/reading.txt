Note: <p><strong><em>truth (n.)</em></strong></p><p>Middle English <em>treuth</em>, <em>truþ</em>, from Old English <em>triewð</em> (West Saxon), <em>treowð</em> (Mercian) "faith, faithfulness; fidelity to country, kin, friends; loyalty; disposition to be faithful; veracity, quality of being true; pledge, covenant."</p><p>This is reconstructed to be from a Germanic abstract noun from Proto-Germanic <em>treuwaz</em> "having or characterized by good faith." This in turn is reconstructed in Watkins to be from PIE <em>drew-o-</em>, a suffixed form of the root <em>deru-</em> "be firm, solid, steadfast." With Germanic abstract noun suffix <em>-itho</em> (see -th (2)). Compare troth, truce, trust (n.), tree (n.). English and most other IE languages do not have a primary verb for "speak the truth," as a contrast to lie (v.).</p><p>The sense of "something that is true, a true statement or proposition" is recorded by mid-14c. The meaning "accuracy, correctness, conformity of thought with fact" is from 1560s. It is attested by late 14c. as "that which is righteous or in accordance with divine standard; true religious doctrine; virtuous conduct." <em>Truth!</em> as an expression of assent or emphasis is by 1530s.</p>
